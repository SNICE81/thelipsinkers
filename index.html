A Celebration of the Camp and the Crazy

★★★★★
Donald Hutera says LipSinkers do not just pay lip service to drag, they take it to a new level

Evolution of the Fags, Liquid Room Annexe Double, Edinburgh Festival

The official word is that there are 3,314 registered shows at the Fringe this year, of which 24 per cent are free (whether ticketed or non-ticketed). Although donations are welcome, you could swim for days in this sea of talent at little or no cost. Thus far I’ve hardly seen any of it. There is, however, one free performance that absolutely floats my boat.

The LipSinkers are a London-based drag quintet comprised of four blokes and a woman who behaves just like her male counterparts. As incredibly manic and skilful practitioners of the art of lip-synching, their musical choices range from obscure and/or vintage Broadway show tunes to pop songs. They’re camp, cult and glorious mainly because they go for broke serving up what amounts to a generous string of full-blown mini-production numbers.

The pace of their show, Evolution of the Fags, is breakneck and the volume loud, with bad wigs galore and discarded costumes (hugely and inventively tacky) and props littering the stage. The LipSinkers offer an outrageously good time via renditions of, say, Ethel Merman’s infamous disco version of There’s No Business Like Show Business; Queen’s Bohemian Rhapsody, complete with pocket-torch facial lighting; or a duet between the late Keith Harris and Orville the Duck reconfigured as a pricelessly twee love song that climaxes with a long snog.

Without wanting to spray these nutty, gifted entertainers with the perfume of pretentiousness, their act also contains its fair share of indirect socio-cultural commentary. The political subtext is most explicit in their take on Jeffrey Lewis & The Rain’s WWPRD, a paean to the Russian feminist anarchists Pussy Riot. They also lend Tinie Tempah’s Pass Out new meaning thanks to a lead vocal delivered by a coked-up woman in a kaftan who’s surrounded by a handful of similarly determined party animals. As a celebration of excessive self-indulgence it can’t be topped. But the entire hour spent with these shamelessly mad, tireless and gender-bending drag clowns builds up a liberating and euphoric energy. Go.

Donald Hutera, The Times, 21 August 2015
